Four filters: eligibility (101), support (112(a)), novelty/obviousness (102/103), clarity (112(b)).

1. Name the technical problem in the computer or network itself (latency, memory, security, accuracy of a sensor pipeline), not a business goal.
2. Claim the specific technical solution: the steps and data structures, not the result.
3. Tie to hardware: processor, memory, camera, network interface, with real roles.
4. Avoid claims that merely say "apply it on a computer".
5. State a measurable improvement in the description; label any numbers as examples and never invent test results.
6. Describe unconventional elements with facts (why the arrangement is not routine); eligibility can depend on fact findings.
7. Method, system and computer-readable medium claims, each non-abstract.
8. Avoid "module for/means for" without structure and algorithm (see means-plus-function-112f).
9. Provide algorithm-level detail: inputs, steps, outputs, flowchart.
10. Search prior art; software is often anticipated by obvious combinations.
11. Check dependents add technical detail, not just business rules.

Reality check: eligibility is the main software risk; courts and examiners are stricter than older advice suggests. Consult counsel.
