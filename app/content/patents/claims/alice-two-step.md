The Supreme Court test from Mayo and Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank (2014):

**Step one**: is the claim "directed to" a judicial exception (abstract idea, law of nature, natural phenomenon)? Courts ask what the claim's focus is, not just whether it mentions an idea.

**Step two**: if yes, do the claim elements, individually and as an ordered combination, add an **inventive concept** that transforms it into a patent-eligible application? Adding a generic computer or "apply it" language does not.

The USPTO folds these into Step 2A (prongs 1 and 2) and Step 2B in MPEP 2106. At Step 2B, whether an element is well-understood, routine, and conventional is a factual question needing evidence (Berkheimer v. HP, Fed. Cir. 2018).

Example: a method of intermediated settlement using a generic computer was held abstract in Alice. In contrast, McRO (specific rules for lip-synch animation) and Enfish (a self-referential table improving computer operation) survived.

Tip: draft the specification to explain a concrete technical problem and solution so you can argue the claim improves technology, not just an idea running on a computer.
