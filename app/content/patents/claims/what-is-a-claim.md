A claim is the numbered sentence at the end of a patent that defines the legal scope of the invention: its "metes and bounds." Think of it as the fence line. What is inside the fence is covered; the rest of the document (specification, drawings) explains and supports the fence but does not itself set it.

Key points:
- Each claim is a single sentence, numbered, ending in a period (37 CFR 1.75).
- Infringement requires that an accused product or method meets every element of a claim (the "all elements" rule).
- Courts read claim words in light of the specification and prosecution history (Phillips v. AWH; Markman v. Westview).
- Claims must be definite (35 USC 112(b)) and supported by the specification (112(a)).
- Many claims means many fences of different sizes; the patent can be infringed by any one of them.

Example: "A device comprising a sensor and a processor configured to..." covers any device with at least those two parts, even if it also has a screen.

Tip: read issued claims (often claim 1, the broadest) before the long description when judging what a patent really covers.
