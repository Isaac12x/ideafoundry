Prosecution laches is an equitable defense: a patent can be unenforceable if the applicant unreasonably and inexcusably delayed prosecution, and the delay prejudiced others who began using the technology.

Key cases:
- Symbol Technologies v. Lemelson (Fed. Cir. 2005): patents made unenforceable for decades of delay by serial refiling.
- Hyatt v. Hirshfeld (Fed. Cir. 2021): the USPTO may itself reject claims for prosecution laches, not only courts after issuance, based on a pattern of delay (hundreds of applications, huge claim sets).

Factors: length of delay, whether it was explained, the applicant's overall conduct, and (in court) intervening rights. Using the continuation procedure itself is lawful; the problem is unreasonable, unexplained delay.

Example (illustrative): refiling a family for many years while waiting for a competitor's product to appear, then claiming it, is the classic risk. Filing a continuation for good reasons while moving the family along steadily is not.

Practical: keep a record of reasons for each filing, advance claims, and avoid open-ended delays.
