Unexpected results are a showing that the claimed invention performs better, or differently, than a skilled person would have predicted. If real, it can rebut an obviousness rejection.

What it must be:
- True and measured: actual test data, not an assertion. Never claim results you have not obtained.
- Compared to the closest prior art, not an easy baseline.
- Unexpected, not merely expected: show that the prior art would not have predicted the result.
- Commensurate with claim scope: data at one narrow setting may not support a broad claim.
- Presented as evidence, typically a declaration under 37 CFR 1.132 by someone with knowledge; lawyer argument alone carries little weight.

Example (illustrative only, not real data): if, in testing, a pre-launch local check reduced page-load delay compared with the closest prior system, and the prior art suggested it would increase delay, the declaration would set out the setup, the comparison, the numbers, and why the result was surprising.

In the specification, describe real effects only. Prophetic examples must be written as prophetic, not as past experiments.
