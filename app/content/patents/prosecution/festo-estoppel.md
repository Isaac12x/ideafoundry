The doctrine of equivalents lets a patent reach products that differ slightly from the literal claim. Prosecution history estoppel limits that: what you gave up to get the patent, you cannot later reclaim through equivalents.

Festo v. Shoketsu (U.S. 2002): a narrowing amendment made for a reason related to patentability (including 112 reasons) presumptively surrenders all territory between the original and amended claim.

The patentee can rebut the presumption by showing that:
1. The equivalent was unforeseeable at the time of the amendment;
2. The rationale for the amendment bore no more than a tangential relation to the equivalent; or
3. There was some other reason the applicant could not reasonably have described the equivalent.

Argument-based estoppel can also arise when you clearly distinguish prior art by argument.

Example (illustrative): claim 1 originally recited "checking a URL". To overcome Smith, you amend to "checking a URL before launching it". Later you cannot easily argue a post-launch check is equivalent.

Practical: weigh each narrowing amendment against future enforcement; prefer filing a continuation to keep broader claims alive.
