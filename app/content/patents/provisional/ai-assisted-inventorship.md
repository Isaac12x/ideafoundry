Rule: an inventor must be a natural person. Thaler v. Vidal (Fed. Cir. 2022) held that the Patent Act's "individual" means a human, so an AI system such as DABUS cannot be named.

AI-assisted inventions are not categorically unpatentable. USPTO guidance (Feb 2024) said a human must make a significant contribution to the conception of each claim, using established joint-inventorship factors. Using AI as a tool does not by itself make or bar inventorship. Later USPTO guidance in 2025 revised this approach (verify the current guidance page): AI is treated as a tool, and conception by a human remains the test.

Practical points:
- Record who conceived what. Prompting alone may not equal conception; recognizing and shaping a solution can.
- Name only humans as inventors; the declaration says so.
- Wrong inventorship can invalidate or make a patent unenforceable, but is correctable if done without deceptive intent and timely.
- Duty of candor applies: if AI output is part of the disclosure, ensure it is checked and enabling. A provisional full of unverified AI text can fail to support claims.
- Eligibility is a separate issue: AI-related claims still face 101 analysis.

Example: you ask a model for design options, pick one, and test and modify it to solve a specific problem. You may be the inventor of the claimed refinement; the tool is not.
