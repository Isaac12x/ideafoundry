Researchers are told to publish early to establish credit. For inventions, publication is also a prior-art event.

What counts as disclosure:
- Papers, preprints (arXiv and the like), slides, blog posts, public code, videos, conference talks.
- Demos, public use, sales or offers for sale (even confidential sales can count in the US).
- Grant proposals or product pages that become public.

Effects:
- US: a one-year grace period for your own disclosures, then a bar.
- Europe and most others: novelty is lost at the moment of public availability.
- Others' later filings can rely on your disclosure as prior art against them but not against you, if you filed first.

Practical sequence:
1. Write the technical disclosure.
2. File a provisional (or non-provisional) that supports every claim you may want.
3. Then publish, demo, or pitch.
4. Within 12 months, file the non-provisional and any foreign or PCT filing.

Confidential sharing (NDA) can be fine, but keep records.

A provisional must describe the matter you disclose: a talk covering extra variants that were not in the provisional gets no protection for those.

Example: a paper submission date is 15 Jan; file the provisional before 15 Jan, not after it is posted.

_Educational, not legal advice._
