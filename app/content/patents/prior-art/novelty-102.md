A claim is anticipated only if a single prior art reference discloses every element of the claim, arranged as in the claim, either expressly or inherently (Verdegaal Bros. v. Union Oil). Miss one element, or the arrangement, and 102 fails; the examiner must then use 103.

Key points:
- Prior art under AIA 102(a) means disclosures before the effective filing date: patents, printed publications, public use, sale, or otherwise available to the public, plus earlier-filed US applications. Exceptions in 102(b) (e.g. the inventor's own disclosure within one year) can remove a reference.
- Inherent means necessarily present, not merely probable.
- The reference must be enabling: a skilled person could make the disclosed thing.
- Changing claim category (method to device) does not avoid a reference that discloses the same subject matter.

Example (illustrative): claim recites scanning a QR code, decoding a URL, and checking it against a malicious-domain list. Reference "Smith" scans and decodes but only displays the URL. The list check is missing, so no anticipation. Check the order too: a reference that checks the URL after launch does not disclose a check before launch.

Use an element-by-element chart: claim limitation, cited passage, present or absent.
