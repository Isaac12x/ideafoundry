KSR International v. Teleflex (U.S. 2007) is the Supreme Court's main modern obviousness case. It rejected a rigid test that required an explicit teaching, suggestion, or motivation in the references.

Holdings in practice:
- Obviousness is an expansive, flexible, common-sense inquiry.
- Combining familiar elements by known methods is likely obvious if it yields predictable results.
- Any need or problem known in the field, not only the patentee's problem, can justify combining references.
- "Obvious to try" can suffice when there are a finite number of predictable options and a reasonable expectation of success.
- Examiners must still give an articulated reason with rational underpinning, and avoid hindsight.

Why "different problem" arguments weaken: saying "Smith solves usability and Johnson solves spam, so no one would combine them" fails if the combination serves any known need. Stronger responses: a missing element, teaching away, no reasonable expectation of success, the combination changes a reference's principle of operation, or unexpected results.

Example (illustrative): a pre-launch URL check added to a QR scanner is likely seen as a predictable use of known safety checks unless the claim recites something more specific and supported.
